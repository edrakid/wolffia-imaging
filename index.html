1. Introduction and value proposition

This project builds a low-cost instrument that photographs Wolffia globosa cultures automatically and follows individual fronds over days, without moving or touching the plates.

1.1. Context

Wolffia globosa is among the fastest-growing flowering plants, doubling in about 48 hours, and contains 20 to 48% protein by dry weight (Rajesh et al., 2026). The wider capstone theme argues that its cultivation is limited by inherited, surface-bound growing methods rather than by the plant itself. Every pathway in that theme, whether multi-layer growth structures, alternative media or growth models, depends on one thing: measuring growth reliably and often.

1.2. Problem

Growth in our collaborator's lab (Noemi) is currently measured by moving culture plates to a camera by hand. This has three costs. First, each move disturbs floating fronds, so the same frond cannot be followed from one image to the next. Second, imaging is limited to when a person is available, so growth curves have few points. Third, published duckweed imaging systems mostly report population totals such as frond area or count, which hide how individual fronds behave.

1.3. Value proposition

The instrument moves the camera to the plates instead of moving the plates to the camera. Because the plates stay still, the system can record each frond's position, size and colour every 15 minutes for a week. That turns imaging from a population count into individual-level, longitudinal data: when each frond buds, how long that takes and how this varies within a clone.

At the interim presentation, the panel argued that the project read as a fix for one user's workflow. They quoted the idea that "science owes more to the steam engine than the steam engine owes to science", meaning that instruments often create the questions science later answers. This report takes that framing on board. The contribution is not the gantry, which is built from known parts, but the new kind of measurement it makes possible. Low cost then becomes experimental capacity, because a lab can run several identical units in parallel (scaling out) where it could afford only one motorised zoom microscope.

Two details from this review shaped the design. Cox et al. (2022) stopped lid condensation by coating lids with dilute Triton X-100 in ethanol, which is a candidate fix for fogging seen in this project. They also expected stationary Raspberry Pi systems to give lower image quality, and section 6 tests exactly that claim.

2.2. Gap and claimed contribution

The gap this project addresses is individual-level, longitudinal data on floating Wolffia from an instrument cheap enough to replicate. Population growth curves are well established. Following each frond through a week, recording when it buds and which daughter came from which mother, is much rarer, because floating fronds drift, crowd and divide.

Neither closest system covers this. Cox et al. (2022) imaged one Lemna minor colony per well on semi-solid medium and measured each colony's total area over 67 hours. They did not follow individual fronds within a colony, record budding or image plants in liquid. Kose et al. (2023) reported population totals only.

Two other studies come closer and narrow the claim. Li et al. (2023) followed individual W. australiana plantlets over time, but only by confining them single file in 1 mm channels, where position records the budding order. This project follows fronds floating freely in ordinary plates. Oyeniran et al. (2026) describe Deepweed, software for segmenting and tracking individual duckweed fronds to build lineages. Its imaging setup will be reviewed for the final report: if it already tracks free-floating fronds from automated imaging, the contribution narrows to the low-cost instrument that produces such data without moving plates.

This report does not claim that gantry imaging, CoreXY motion, saturation thresholding or watershed segmentation are new. Each is an existing technique, and the contribution lies in combining them to produce data that was not previously practical to collect.

The drift data (section 6.3) also show a pattern worth separating out. Fronds within about 5 mm of a wall moved towards it at about 0.043 mm/h, more than twice the 0.018 mm/h further in. This could be the "Cheerios effect", where the curved water surface at a wall (the meniscus) pulls floating objects towards it through surface tension. However, fronds across the whole plate also drifted in one direction, which points to a slight tilt of the plate as the main cause. Subtracting the plate-wide drift before measuring movement towards the walls would show how much of the edge effect is the meniscus.


3. Aim, hypotheses and scope

The aim is to build and evaluate a low-cost gantry imager that detects and tracks individual Wolffia fronds on four stationary plates over a 7-day run, and to report where the method stops working.

3.1. Hypotheses

Each hypothesis has a baseline to beat and a measure that could prove it wrong. Thresholds are set from early measurements before the main runs, so the targets are not chosen after seeing the results.

A Kruskal-Wallis test compares groups using the ranks of the values instead of the values themselves. It suits displacement and budding data, which are skewed (most fronds barely move, a few move a lot), so an average would mislead.

If H3 or H4 fails, that is still a useful result: it tells the lab that fixed cameras, one per plate, are the better design for this task. Earlier proposals also included hypotheses on image stitching and autofocus during evaporation. Both were dropped once one frame per plate proved to give enough resolution (section 6.3) and focus was locked to keep magnification constant.

3.2. Scope

In scope

  • A CoreXY gantry carrying a Raspberry Pi camera over four plates on a fixed tray
  • Capture, detection and tracking software, released as open source
  • Validation of each part against a baseline (H1 to H4)
  • At least one full 7-day run on four plates, plus a replicated strain comparison (H5)

Out of scope

  • Any claim that results transfer to other species or growth media without testing
  • Biological conclusions beyond what the measured budding intervals and growth rates show
  • Automatic tool changing during a run, which Jubilee and the Duckbot already do
  • Closed-loop intervention such as frond removal, except as a stretch goal (section 7.4)

5.3. System design

The system has four parts, and each passes data to the next:

  1. Motion. THE 100 frame with the hotend, extruder and heated bed left out, which saves cost and keeps heat away from the plates. The Pi sends G-code moves (the text commands 3D printers understand) to Klipper. Only X and Y are homed during a run, because homing Z on THE 100 would move the bed and the plates. Moves use low acceleration and a settle delay before each photo.
  2. Imaging head. A printed mount holds the Camera Module 3 at the measured working height of 12 cm. Exposure, gain, white balance and focus are all locked, so frames differ only because the plants changed. Auto exposure would shift colours and break the detector's thresholds, and autofocus would change magnification and therefore measured sizes.
  3. Plate tray. A printed tray holds four plates level and at the same height, so that one locked focus suits every plate. THE 100 levels its bed by hand, so the tray will be levelled at setup and checked with a short drift run, since section 6.3 showed that a slight tilt makes fronds slide. It also carries a ruler for scale and printed crosses (fiducials) as fixed reference points for measuring drift.
  4. Software. A scheduler (cron) triggers one cycle every 15 minutes: visit each plate, capture a 12 MP still and log its time and sequence number. Detection runs afterwards and writes one row per frond to fronds.csv, flagging fronds cut by the image edge so later analyses can include or exclude them.

A Raspberry Pi 4B controls the gantry and runs from mains power through the cable chain, because the first long run showed a power bank lasts only about 30 hours.

5.4. Modular interfaces

In response to the panel's question about scale, the head and tray are designed as modules with defined interfaces now, while parts are still being printed. A module here is a part that can be swapped without redesigning the rest, because the points where it connects are fixed. There are four interfaces: a standard mechanical mount on the head, a standard electrical connector down the cable chain, a common software command for "perform your action now" and a shared data format keyed by plate and time. This lets a higher-resolution camera, a fluorescence camera or a different plate format (6-well or 24-well) be fitted later. Tools are swapped by hand between runs using a kinematic mount, which locates a part with balls sitting in grooves so it returns to the same position every time. That is simpler than Jubilee's automatic tool changing, at the cost of one tool per run.

Motion firmware. The gantry runs Klipper, the open-source firmware THE 100 is designed for, and the Pi sends it standard G-code. Writing custom motion firmware was considered but would add roughly 7 to 11 weeks, so it is left out to keep the time on imaging, tracking and the 7-day runs.

5.5. Detection pipeline

Each image is cropped to the plate interior, pulled about 2 mm in from the wall so the wall and the clumps along it are excluded. The pipeline then runs four steps:

  1. Convert to the HSV colour space and keep the saturation channel, where green fronds stand out from the pale background.
  2. Threshold with Otsu's method, which picks the cut-off automatically from each image's own histogram, so it adapts to small lighting changes.
  3. Separate touching fronds with a two-threshold watershed, which treats the image like a landscape and floods it from markers placed inside each frond.
  4. Merge pieces that are probably two halves of one frond: adjacent pieces whose combined area is close to one median frond and whose combined shape is solid (convex).

6. Prototype fabrication, test methods and preliminary results

A fixed-stand prototype validated the camera, capture and detector before the gantry was built, so any problem found later can be traced to the gantry alone.

6.1. Fabrication of the fixed-stand prototype

The camera stand was designed in OpenSCAD and printed on a Bambu Lab A1. OpenSCAD describes parts in code, so the design is parametric: changing one setting, such as the dish diameter, resizes the part without redrawing it. The stand has a pocket that puts the plate in the same place every time, a ledge for a ruler (scale) and printed crosses used as fixed references for stability tests. A 10-minute test print of the pocket alone caught a 7 mm sizing error before the full 6-hour print.

The stand carries a Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W and a Camera Module 3, with the settings locked as described in section 5.3.

The resolution result answers the expectation in Cox et al. (2022) that stationary Pi systems give lower image quality. At whole-plate framing every camera spreads its pixels over the same 75 mm, so the Canon gave only about 1.5 times more pixels per frond. Its sharper look came mostly from lens and sensor quality, which only matters if hand counts show errors caused by missing detail.

A possible stretch goal is closed-loop control, where the frond count feeds back into an action on each plate. The simplest first action is topping up medium to replace evaporation, which needs only a small pump. Removing fronds to hold each plate at a set density would be the headline demonstration, but it raises hard problems: a removal tool for 1 mm fronds, disturbance after each intervention and keeping cultures sterile.


8. Conclusion

The camera side of the system is validated, which was the main technical risk of the gantry approach. On a fixed stand, the Pi camera gives about 27 to 30 px per frond, captured 102 of 102 scheduled frames and stayed within 1 px over 15 hours. The detector reached 92% recall and 93% precision on images it was never tuned on. Six of the ten specifications are met.

The remaining risk lies in the gantry: whether a printed CoreXY frame returns to each plate repeatably (H3) and whether its moves leave floating fronds undisturbed (H4). Both have defined tests, baselines and a fallback design, so either outcome produces a usable result. If both hold, the 7-day runs in semester 2 will produce individual-level growth data on floating Wolffia from an instrument a lab can afford to replicate.
